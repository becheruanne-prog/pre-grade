# Frameworks used by pre-grade

All content below is paraphrased from the primary sources linked in each section (read 25 Nov 2026). Short quotations are marked and were checked against the source text. Use the vocabulary; do not reproduce the source documents.

## 1. Cambridge Assessment critical thinking taxonomy

Source: Black, Chislett, Thomson, Thwaites & Thwaites (2008), "Critical Thinking: a definition and taxonomy for Cambridge Assessment", Research Matters 6, pp. 30-35. https://www.cambridgeassessment.org.uk/Images/480038-critical-thinking-a-definition-and-taxonomy-for-cambridge-assessment.pdf
This taxonomy sits behind Cambridge Assessment's critical thinking tests, including the TSA used in Oxford and Cambridge admissions.

In short: critical thinking is the careful, rigorous analysis that underlies reasoned argument. It covers analysing arguments, judging how relevant and significant information is, evaluating claims and inferences, building coherent arguments, reaching well-reasoned judgements, and being open-minded yet critical about one's own thinking. An argument is one or more reasons offered for a conclusion.

Codes used in reports:
- 1 Analysis: 1A uses reasoning vocabulary · 1B tells argument from explanation or description · 1C recognises types of reasoning (statistics, principles, conditionals, data) · 1D separates key claims from background and rhetoric · 1E identifies the structure and the role of each part · 1F finds unstated assumptions · 1G clarifies ambiguous meaning
- 2 Evaluation: 2A relevance (degree, not yes/no) · 2B sufficiency (enough evidence; necessary vs sufficient) · 2C significance · 2D credibility of sources (expertise, corroboration, bias) · 2E plausibility, including alternative explanations · 2F analogies · 2G errors in reasoning and fallacies · 2H overall soundness · 2I effect of further evidence
- 3 Inference: 3A implications and internal consistency · 3B conclusion justified by what came before
- 4 Synthesis: 4A selects relevant, sufficient material · 4B builds a coherent argument or counter-argument · 4C takes an argument further · 4D well-reasoned judgement where evidence is incomplete · 4E dilemmas · 4F justified decisions
- 5 Self-reflection: 5A questions own preconceptions · 5B evaluates own reasoning

Cambridge places these outside critical thinking: reading comprehension (summary), problem solving, creativity for its own sake, knowledge of research methods or ethical theories by themselves.

## 2. AAC&U Critical Thinking VALUE Rubric

Source: AAC&U, https://www.aacu.org/value-rubrics (copy read at https://assessment.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk5396/files/files/page/VALUE_Rubric-Critical_Thinking.pdf). AAC&U intends it for assessing learning across an institution, not for grading individual work. It defines critical thinking as exploring issues, ideas and evidence thoroughly before accepting or forming a conclusion. Levels: 4 Capstone, 3 and 2 Milestones, 1 Benchmark, 0 below Benchmark. Read the original before relying on exact boundaries.

Level guide (paraphrased):

Explanation of issues
- 4: issue stated clearly and described fully, with everything needed to understand it
- 3: stated and clarified; omissions do not seriously get in the way
- 2: stated, but terms undefined, ambiguities unexplored or boundaries unclear
- 1: stated with no clarification

Evidence
- 4: sources interpreted and evaluated enough for a thorough analysis; expert views questioned thoroughly
- 3: enough interpretation for a coherent analysis; expert views questioned
- 2: some interpretation, not enough for coherence; experts mostly taken as fact
- 1: sources used without interpretation; experts taken as fact

Influence of context and assumptions
- 4: systematically analyses own and others' assumptions and weighs relevant contexts
- 3: identifies own and others' assumptions and several contexts
- 2: questions some assumptions; may see others' more than own, or the reverse
- 1: emerging awareness; may label assertions as assumptions

Student's position (thesis)
- 4: imaginative, takes complexity into account, admits its limits, synthesises other views
- 3: takes complexity into account; acknowledges other views
- 2: acknowledges different sides
- 1: stated but underdeveloped or obvious

Conclusions and related outcomes
- 4: logical, reflect informed evaluation, rank evidence and perspectives by importance
- 3: tied to a range of information including opposing views; implications clear
- 2: tied to information chosen to fit the conclusion; some implications clear
- 1: loosely tied to the information; implications oversimplified

## 3. Harvard / Princeton: Gordon Harvey, "Elements of the Academic Essay"

Sources: Harvard College Writing Program, Harvard Writing Project Brief Guide, "A Brief Guide to the Elements of the Academic Essay", copyright 2009 Gordon Harvey (copy at https://www.ccsu.edu/sites/default/files/document/hwp_brief_guides_elements.pdf). Earlier 2003 version hosted on princeton.edu: https://www.princeton.edu/~abw/elementsrev.doc. The guide says Harvard writing instructors tend to use this vocabulary when commenting on student writing.

1. Thesis: the main proposition. True but arguable, limited in scope, central to the topic, stated early and recast sharply, present throughout.
2. Motive: why an intelligent reader would need this thesis argued: a genuine puzzle, misreading or tension, set up in the introduction.
3. Keyterms: the few recurring concepts the argument rests on. Clear, used consistently, not a false opposition or cliché.
4. Evidence: enough, the right kind, concrete, fairly summarised, nothing obvious left out.
5. Analysis: interpreting the evidence so it supports the thesis; more analysis than summary. Includes the logic connecting evidence to claim and the assumptions behind it.
6. Structure: clear, logical, progressive sections; development, not repeated restatement.
7. Stitching: transitions and echoed key terms that join the parts.
8. Sources: integrated efficiently and credited fairly.
9. Reflecting: handling objections, defining terms, drawing implications, explaining causes, qualifying the claim.
10. Orienting: just enough background for a non-expert reader.
11. Stance: a consistent position toward reader and subject.
12. Style: exact and clear, plain without being flat.

Princeton Writing Program lexicon (https://tortoise.princeton.edu/lexicon) uses the same core terms and adds Method: how the evidence is analysed, chosen to fit the question.

## 4. University marking criteria: Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, Princeton

What these four universities publish for marking essays and dissertations. All four use grade bands (Oxford and Cambridge: First 70+, 2:1 60-69, 2:2 50-59, Third 40-49; Harvard and Princeton: letter grades). pre-grade uses the criteria, never the bands.

Shared criteria, in the universities' own terms:

1. Answering the question (Oxford, Cambridge). Cambridge History calls the extent to which the candidate addresses the question "most fundamental". Oxford Law puts close attention to the question into its descriptors.
   Check: does the paper answer the question set, or a nearby one it preferred?
2. Argument and thesis (all four). Oxford History: "coherence, control, and independence of argument". Cambridge History: quality of the argument, which can be bold and does not have to be right. Harvard's sample rubric marks down a thesis "more descriptive than arguable". Princeton English wants a "strong, even adventurous, thesis"; the Princeton Writing Program ties the thesis to its motive.
   Check: is there an arguable thesis that controls the paper?
3. Evidence and analysis (all four). Oxford and Cambridge: accurate, relevant, wide-ranging evidence, critically examined. Harvard and Princeton: well-chosen evidence that is analysed, not just described or summarised; summary in place of analysis marks lower bands.
   Check: is evidence interpreted, or only reported?
4. Independence of thought (all four, strongest at Oxford and Cambridge). Oxford names independence of argument at the top band; Princeton English asks writers to go beyond the expected.
   Check: does the paper go beyond restating its sources?
5. Engagement with scholarship and counter-arguments (all four). Oxford History: awareness of the historiography and alternative interpretations. Cambridge: engagement with debates and recognising limitations. Harvard's sample rubric rewards taking on counterarguments and alternative interpretations.
   Check: does the paper know the debate and answer the strongest objection?
6. Structure (all four). Oxford: clarity and coherence. Cambridge: penalises structure dictated by the available information rather than the argument. Harvard's sample rubric: penalises formulaic structure (such as the five-paragraph essay).
   Check: does the order follow the argument?
7. Expression and referencing (all four, always a minor criterion). Clear, precise prose and correct citation.

Distinctive to one or two:
- Motive (Princeton Writing Program, Princeton English): why the thesis matters. Harvard's "stakes" are the nearest equivalent.
- Method (Princeton Writing Program; Princeton Economics for theses).
- Problem definition for theses and dissertations (Oxford, Cambridge).

## Note on "Harvard style"
"Harvard referencing" is an author-date citation format with no official connection to Harvard University and no single official manual (https://www.zotero.org/support/kb/harvard_style). When a teacher says "Harvard standard", check whether they mean this citation format or Harvey's Elements.
